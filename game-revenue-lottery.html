Looks Like I Bought a Lottery Ticket: How I See Making Money from My Own Games

Oct 2026Games · Making things

At university, I didn't worry much about my grades. I went to classes, did other things on the side, and got whatever grades I got. To be fair, they were good or excellent, but I didn't think about them much. Then, as graduation got closer, I realized I could graduate with honors. I got really caught up in the idea. I arranged to retake a couple of exams (history had been a struggle), studied hard for my final exams, and got busy sorting out paperwork and grade requirements. One day, a classmate asked, “Why are you putting in so much effort?” I explained that I was only half a point short, and that would get me a special red honors diploma and some extra gift (which later turned out to be a watch from the regional governor, in a lovely box). He said, “Oh, you like unlocking achievements.” And just like that, he told me something true about myself.

I like unlocking achievements.

I don't think I'm alone. You follow a clear set of rules and get a badge. You don't even have to show it to anyone. What matters is that you have it: a milestone, proof that you did something, a step forward. Call it whatever you like. It makes sense, and it feels good. Even when the requirements are hard and take a lot of money, time, or energy, there's a badge waiting at the end. Usually, the badge even feels worth the effort.

Life has achievements to unlock, too. In a way, your salary or job title can be one: you've reached a certain level, and here's the proof. Or take a more tangible kind of badge. Say you work hard, save up, and finally buy a small apartment to rent out. That takes effort, money, time, and stress, too. At the end, you have an apartment. A very tangible badge for all that effort.

Making money from your own app or game works differently. Even if you already have the education and experience, as I did, you still have to put in time and energy and learn new things along the way. You use everything you know and get the game released. Whether it earns anything is another question. I once did some rough calculations for my game: with banner ads and a global eCPM of around $0.30 to $0.50, you'd need about 300 to 500 daily active users just to scrape together $10 a month. That's an average scenario.

Broader figures paint a similar picture. According to Sensor Tower’s State of Mobile 2026 report, the top 1% of publishers took around 92% of in-app purchase revenue on iOS and Google Play in 2025. Advertising revenue isn't included in that figure. These numbers cover a different revenue model and a much wider market than my game, but they show how concentrated that market is: a very small group of apps takes most of the money.

This is where I draw the line: releasing a game can count as an achievement. Making a profit from it feels more like a lottery. I don't like lotteries. Publishing has requirements I can meet. With money, I can't just work through a checklist and collect my reward. All that effort gives me a chance that the game will find its audience and start earning money.

I came to this lottery with an education, years of development experience, and an understanding of how products work. I wasn't learning everything from scratch for my game. With that background, you'd think I could just get on with it. Even so, I had to put time and energy into the game, figure out unfamiliar things, and get it ready for release. For all that work, I got a ticket. A chance to make money.

The apartment makes for an interesting comparison. There's an entry cost there, too: you need the money to buy one, or you need to inherit one. Then the paths split. You buy an apartment, and you have an apartment. You can live in it yourself, try renting it out, or sell it later. You build an app, and you've put in time and effort, used your skills, and made a finished product. Whether it earns money comes down to the draw. You can do everything right and still walk away with two cents.

What It Feels Like from the Inside

Building your own app is a long series of small decisions spread over months. You decide what to make, plan the architecture, write the code, test it, catch bugs that only show up on real devices, set up monetization, pass the app store review, and publish. Then there's silence, and you keep refreshing the dashboard to see whether anyone has shown up. Every step takes real skills, and you put them to use properly, without cutting corners. None of those steps buys you an audience. You can write flawless code and end up with no users at all. The connection between the quality of your work and the outcome is weaker than you'd like to think.

Why Does It Work This Way?

I think there's a fairly simple explanation in how apartments and apps work. It helps me make sense of this beyond just luck or the unfairness of the world.

An apartment has room for one household. Everyone looking for a place in a city physically can't move into the single best apartment. Demand has to spread across many properties because space is limited. So an average apartment can almost always find a tenant if the price is reasonable.

For an app, the cost of serving one more user is close to zero. A single app can serve even a billion people (yes, yes, I know servers and scaling cost money). That means demand can concentrate in a handful of leading apps, and it does. People download what everyone else has downloaded, what comes up first in search, and what their friends recommend. That ability to keep adding users is what creates a winner-takes-all market.

To be fair, the rental market doesn't guarantee a tenant for every apartment either. There are towns and villages where demand is so low that properties can sit empty for years. The point is the physical limit on how many people a property can accommodate. That limit shapes how demand is spread across properties. An app has much more room to grow.

So here I am at the lottery counter. I've paid the entry fee and bought my ticket. All this time, I thought I liked achievements: clear rules and a guaranteed result once I'd met the requirements. I don't know whether I would have come here if I'd understood the rules in advance. Maybe I would have. There's a thrill to this, too, just a different kind from unlocking achievements. Well, since I'm here, okay, I'll take another ticket. I can't give you advice on how to win. I'm still figuring this out, with a ticket in my hand. You're welcome to try your luck alongside me.
